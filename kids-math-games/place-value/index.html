What kids practice

  • Ones, tens and hundreds — what each digit in a number is worth
  • Building and reading numbers to 100 (1st grade) and 1,000 (2nd grade)
  • Trading 10 ones for 1 ten, and 10 tens for 1 hundred
  • Writing numbers in expanded form

Try one together: what is 347 made of?

  1. The 3 is in the hundreds place: 3 hundreds = 300.
  2. The 4 is in the tens place: 4 tens = 40.
  3. The 7 is in the ones place: 7 ones = 7.
  4. 347 = 300 + 40 + 7.

Tips for parents and teachers

  • Bundle real things — straws, craft sticks — into groups of 10 with a rubber band.
  • Ask “what is this digit worth?” rather than “what digit is this?”
  • Strong place value is the secret behind carrying, borrowing and long multiplication.

Matching school standards

These games line up with these Common Core State Standards for Mathematics:

  • 1.NBT.B.2 Understand the two digits of a two-digit number as tens and ones.
  • 2.NBT.A.1 Understand the three digits of a three-digit number as hundreds, tens and ones.
  • 2.NBT.A.3 Read and write numbers to 1,000, including expanded form.

Questions parents ask

Why is place value so important?

Almost every written math method — carrying, borrowing, long multiplication and long division — relies on knowing what each digit is worth. Kids who are solid on place value find those methods make sense.

What grade learns place value?

Tens and ones start in 1st grade, hundreds in 2nd grade, and place value keeps growing to thousands, millions and decimals through 5th grade.
