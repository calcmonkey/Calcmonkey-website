Try one together: 7 × 8 by breaking it apart

  1. 7 × 8 is the same as 5 × 8 plus 2 × 8.
  2. 5 × 8 = 40.
  3. 2 × 8 = 16.
  4. 40 + 16 = 56, so 7 × 8 = 56.

Tips for parents and teachers

  • Learn 2s, 5s and 10s first, then 3s, 4s and 9s, then the tricky 6s, 7s and 8s.
  • Turnaround facts halve the work: 3 × 8 is the same as 8 × 3.
  • Nines trick: the digits of 9 × n add up to 9 (9 × 4 = 36, and 3 + 6 = 9).

Questions parents ask

When should kids know their times tables?

Most standards expect kids to know all products of two one-digit numbers from memory by the end of 3rd grade. Many keep practicing through 12 × 12 in 4th and 5th grade.

What order should kids learn the times tables in?

A common order is 2s, 5s and 10s, then 3s, 4s and 9s, then 6s, 7s and 8s. Each new table can be built from ones already learned.
